CLAIMSET Complex exponential smoothing
summary Exponential smoothing forecasts a series by first choosing a model: with or without a trend, with or without seasonality. This paper proposes complex exponential smoothing (CES), which uses complex variables, pairing each value with its forecast error, so that one model moves smoothly between a level and a trend as its smoothing parameter changes. It shows that CES can be stationary or non-stationary depending on that parameter, and that it corresponds to an ARMA(2,2) model whose coefficients are tied together. A seasonal version handles seasonality without seasonal indices, so model selection reduces to a choice between two models. On the series of the M1, M3, tourism and M4 forecasting competitions, CES was more accurate than exponential smoothing, ARIMA and the Theta method on most error measures, though not on all, and adding it to a combination of forecasts improved the combination.

DEFINITIONS
D1 [method] CES: Complex exponential smoothing: a forecasting method that combines each past observation $y_t$ and its forecast error $e_t$ into the complex variable $y_t + i e_t$ and forecasts with an exponentially weighted sum of these values, $$\hat{y}_t + i\hat{e}_t = (\alpha_0 + i\alpha_1) \sum_{j=1}^{t-1} (1 + i - (\alpha_0 + i\alpha_1))^{j-1} (y_{t-j} + i e_{t-j})$$, or recursively $$\hat{y}_t + i\hat{e}_t = (\alpha_0 + i\alpha_1)(y_{t-1} + i e_{t-1}) + (1 - \alpha_0 + i - i\alpha_1)(\hat{y}_{t-1} + i\hat{e}_{t-1})$$, where $t$ indexes the observations, $i$ is the imaginary unit ($i^2 = -1$), $\hat{y}_t$ is the forecast of $y_t$, $j$ indexes the past observation $y_{t-j}$, $\alpha_0 + i\alpha_1$ is the complex smoothing parameter and $\hat{e}_t$ carries the information on past forecast errors. [assumes C60, C62, C63, C64]
D2 [variable] complex smoothing parameter ($\alpha_0 + i\alpha_1$): The smoothing parameter of [[D1:CES]], the complex number $\alpha_0 + i\alpha_1$; its real part $\alpha_0$ and imaginary part $\alpha_1$ are the two real parameters estimated, and they set how much of the past observations and of the past forecast errors is carried into the forecast. [part of D1]
D3 [concept] nonlinear trend component ($c_t$): The state $c_t$ of the [[D1:CES]] state-space model, which carries information on both its own previous value $c_{t-1}$ and the previous level $l_{t-1}$; it does not correspond to the trend component of exponential smoothing. [part of D1]
D4 [method] seasonal CES: [[D1:CES]] extended with a second pair of level and [[D3:nonlinear trend]] states taken at lag $m$, the seasonal period, with its own [[D2:complex smoothing parameter]] $\beta_0 + i\beta_1$: $$\begin{aligned} y_t &= l_{0,t-1} + l_{1,t-m} + \varepsilon_t \\ l_{0,t} &= l_{0,t-1} - (1 - \alpha_1) c_{0,t-1} + (\alpha_0 - \alpha_1) \varepsilon_t \\ c_{0,t} &= l_{0,t-1} + (1 - \alpha_0) c_{0,t-1} + (\alpha_0 + \alpha_1) \varepsilon_t \\ l_{1,t} &= l_{1,t-m} - (1 - \beta_1) c_{1,t-m} + (\beta_0 - \beta_1) \varepsilon_t \\ c_{1,t} &= l_{1,t-m} + (1 - \beta_0) c_{1,t-m} + (\beta_0 + \beta_1) \varepsilon_t \end{aligned}$$ where $\varepsilon_t$ is the error term, $l_{0,t}$ and $c_{0,t}$ are the level and nonlinear trend of the non-seasonal part, $l_{1,t}$ and $c_{1,t}$ those of the seasonal part, and $\beta_0$ and $\beta_1$ are the real and imaginary parts of $\beta_0 + i\beta_1$. [a kind of D1]

CLAIMS
== 1. The CES model and its state-space form
C16 "State-space form of CES" [formal; principal; support: formal_proof E1; assumes C60, C61; verification agent_checked; verifiability open]
  [[D1:CES]] has the single-source-of-error state-space form $$\begin{aligned} y_t &= l_{t-1} + \varepsilon_t \\ l_t &= l_{t-1} - (1 - \alpha_1) c_{t-1} + (\alpha_0 - \alpha_1) \varepsilon_t \\ c_t &= l_{t-1} + (1 - \alpha_0) c_{t-1} + (\alpha_0 + \alpha_1) \varepsilon_t \end{aligned}$$ with level $l_t$ and [[D3:nonlinear trend component]] $c_t$.
  in plain words: CES can be written as a state-space model with a single source of error and two states: a level and a nonlinear trend component.
  extends B14, own earlier work: Exponential smoothing methods can be written as state-space models with a single source of error.
C41 "The CES transition matrix contains the smoothing parameters" [formal; secondary; support: formal_proof E1; uses C16; verification agent_checked; verifiability open]
  The transition matrix of the [[D1:CES]] state-space form contains the smoothing parameters.
  in plain words: Unlike conventional exponential smoothing, CES has its smoothing parameters in the transition matrix of its state-space form.
== 2. Stationarity and stability of CES
C18 "An imaginary part above one gives a real eigenvalue above one" [formal; supporting; support: formal_proof E3; verification agent_checked; verifiability open]
  When $\alpha_1 > 1$, both eigenvalues of the [[D1:CES]] transition matrix are real and one of them is greater than one.
  in plain words: When the imaginary part of the complex smoothing parameter exceeds one, both eigenvalues of the CES transition matrix are real and one of them is greater than one.
C19 "An imaginary part above one gives a non-stationary trajectory" [formal; supporting; support: formal_proof E3; uses C18; verification agent_checked; verifiability open]
  When $\alpha_1 > 1$, [[D1:CES]] produces a non-stationary trajectory.
  in plain words: When the imaginary part of the complex smoothing parameter exceeds one, CES generates a non-stationary path.
C20 "An imaginary part of one makes CES equal to ETS(A,N,N)" [formal; supporting; support: formal_proof E3; verification agent_checked; verifiability open]
  When $\alpha_1 = 1$, [[D1:CES]] is equivalent to [[D19:ETS(A,N,N)]].
  in plain words: When the imaginary part of the complex smoothing parameter equals one, CES is the local level model of exponential smoothing, ETS(A,N,N).
C17 "Stationarity conditions of CES" [formal; supporting; support: formal_proof E3, cited B1, at p. 38, own earlier work; verification agent_checked; verifiability open]
  [[D1:CES]] is stationary, with both eigenvalues of its transition matrix inside the unit circle, when $\alpha_1 < 5 - 2\alpha_0$, $\alpha_1 < 1$ and $\alpha_1 > 1 - \alpha_0$.
  in plain words: CES is stationary when its two smoothing parameters satisfy three linear inequalities; both eigenvalues of its transition matrix are then smaller than one in absolute value.
C1 "CES is stationary or non-stationary depending on its parameter" [formal; principal; extension; support: formal_proof E3; uses C17, C18, C19, C20, C23, C24, C25, C32, C21, C22, C33; verification agent_checked; verifiability open]
  Depending on the value of its [[D2:complex smoothing parameter]], [[D1:CES]] is a stationary or a non-stationary model.
  in plain words: Whether CES describes a stationary process, one that fluctuates around a fixed mean, or a non-stationary one, one that drifts or trends, depends on the value of its complex smoothing parameter, which is estimated from the data.
  extends B1, own earlier work: Exponential smoothing in a state space framework (ETS) gives 30 models with no, additive or multiplicative error, trend and seasonal components, among which a model is selected by an information criterion.
  addresses B3, own earlier work: Identifying the presence and type of trend in exponential smoothing often leads to misidentification and poorly performing long-term forecasts.
  addresses B2: The diverse trend alternatives of exponential smoothing in a state space framework make the selection of a model difficult.
  addresses B4: Selecting the most appropriate exponential smoothing model is a challenging task.
  addresses B5, own earlier work: Even when the components of exponential smoothing are identified correctly, the estimated smoothing parameters and the weights they imply can give poor forecasts.
C2 "CES passes continuously between stationary and non-stationary" [formal; principal; support: formal_proof E3; uses C17, C18, C25; verification agent_checked; verifiability open]
  [[D1:CES]] passes continuously from a stationary to a non-stationary model as its [[D2:complex smoothing parameter]] changes.
  in plain words: As its complex smoothing parameter changes, CES moves smoothly from a stationary to a non-stationary model, so no switch between separate models is needed.
  extends B1, own earlier work: The exponential smoothing models of the ETS framework are non-stationary.
C22 "CES need not be stationary" [methodological; supporting; support: cited B14, own earlier work, cited B1, own earlier work; verifiability open]
  [[D1:CES]] is not required to be stationary.
  in plain words: Stationarity is not imposed on CES: its smoothing parameters are not restricted to the region where it is stationary.
  scope: data_type: univariate time series | conditions: estimation of the complex smoothing parameter
C21 "CES must be stable, or at least forecastable" [methodological; supporting; support: cited B14, own earlier work, cited B1, own earlier work; verifiability open]
  [[D1:CES]] is required to be stable, or at least forecastable.
  in plain words: Like exponential smoothing models, CES is required to be stable, so that older observations get smaller weights, or at least forecastable.
  scope: data_type: univariate time series | conditions: estimation of the complex smoothing parameter
C23 "Stability conditions of CES" [formal; supporting; support: formal_proof E4, cited B1, at pp. 172-173, own earlier work; verification agent_checked; verifiability open]
  [[D1:CES]] is stable, with all eigenvalues of its discount matrix inside the unit circle, when $(\alpha_0 - 2.5)^2 + \alpha_1^2 > 1.25$, $(\alpha_0 - 0.5)^2 + (\alpha_1 - 1)^2 > 0.25$ and $(\alpha_0 - 1.5)^2 + (\alpha_1 - 0.5)^2 < 1.5$.
  in plain words: CES is stable, with the weights of older observations declining, when its two smoothing parameters lie in a region bounded by three circles.
C24 "Stable CES can be stationary or non-stationary" [formal; supporting; support: formal_proof E4; uses C17, C23; verification agent_checked; verifiability open]
  The stable region of [[D1:CES]] contains both stationary and non-stationary values of the [[D2:complex smoothing parameter]].
  in plain words: The values of the complex smoothing parameter for which CES is stable include values for which it is stationary and values for which it is not.
== 3. Conditional mean, variance and forecast trajectories
C25 "Five forecast trajectories of CES" [formal; supporting; support: formal_proof E3; uses C26, C27, C28, C29, C30; verification agent_checked; verifiability open]
  For $0 < \alpha_0 < 2$ and almost every state, the [[D1:CES]] point forecast $w'F^{h-1}v_t$ for $h$ steps ahead, where $v_t$ is the state vector $(l_t, c_t)'$, $F$ the transition matrix and $w$ the measurement vector of its state-space form, eventually follows one of five trajectories set by the [[D2:complex smoothing parameter]]: a flat line, exponential growth, exponential decline, harmonic oscillation converging to zero, or diverging harmonic oscillation, except on the line $\alpha_0 + \alpha_1 = 1$, where it oscillates with constant amplitude, and on the curve $\alpha_1 = (4 - \alpha_0^2)/4$, where the eigenvalue $\lambda$ of $F$ is repeated, it goes to zero as $(A + Bh)\lambda^h$, with constants $A$ and $B$ set by the state, without oscillating.
  in plain words: For real parts of the complex smoothing parameter between 0 and 2, the CES forecast eventually follows one of five shapes: a flat line, exponential growth, exponential decline, an oscillation dying out, or a growing oscillation; two boundary cases differ.
  explains C36
  explains C37
  corrected from the published paper (correction; Section 3.3, the list of forecasting trajectories): printed There are several types of forecasting trajectories of CES depending on the particular value of the complex smoothing parameter: a flat line, exponential growth, exponential decline, a harmonic trajectory converging to zero and a diverging harmonic trajectory.
C26 "Flat forecast when the imaginary part equals one" [formal; supporting; support: formal_proof E3; verification agent_checked; verifiability open]
  When $\alpha_1 = 1$, the [[D1:CES]] point forecast is a flat line.
  in plain words: When the imaginary part of the complex smoothing parameter equals one, CES forecasts the same value for every future period.
C27 "Exponentially growing forecast when the imaginary part exceeds one" [formal; supporting; support: formal_proof E3; verification agent_checked; verifiability open]
  When $\alpha_1 > 1$ and $\alpha_0 < 2$, the [[D1:CES]] point forecast eventually grows exponentially in magnitude, upward or downward according to the current state, unless the state lies on the eigenvector of the smaller eigenvalue of $F$.
  in plain words: When the imaginary part of the complex smoothing parameter exceeds one and the real part is below two, the CES forecast eventually grows exponentially in magnitude, upward or downward.
  corrected from the published paper (correction; Section 3.3, the list of forecasting trajectories): printed When $\alpha_1 > 1$ the model produces trajectory with exponential growth.
C28 "Exponentially declining forecast for imaginary parts just below one" [formal; supporting; support: formal_proof E3; verification agent_checked; verifiability open]
  When $(4 - \alpha_0^2)/4 < \alpha_1 < 1$, $0 < \alpha_0 < 4$ and $\alpha_1 < 5 - 2\alpha_0$, the [[D1:CES]] point forecast declines exponentially in magnitude and, when $\alpha_0 > 2$, eventually alternates in sign, unless the state lies on the eigenvector of the eigenvalue of $F$ of smaller modulus.
  in plain words: When the imaginary part of the complex smoothing parameter lies below one but above a bound set by the real part, and the real part is between 0 and 4 within the stationarity region, the CES forecast declines exponentially in magnitude, alternating in sign when the real part exceeds two.
  corrected from the published paper (correction; Section 3.3, the list of forecasting trajectories): printed When $(4 - \alpha_0^2)/4 < \alpha_1 < 1$ trajectory becomes stationary and CES produces exponential decline.
C29 "Oscillating forecast converging to zero for intermediate imaginary parts" [formal; supporting; support: formal_proof E3; verification agent_checked; verifiability open]
  When $1 - \alpha_0 < \alpha_1 < (4 - \alpha_0^2)/4$, the [[D1:CES]] point forecast is a harmonic oscillation converging to zero.
  in plain words: For imaginary parts of the complex smoothing parameter in a middle range set by the real part, the CES forecast oscillates with shrinking swings towards zero.
C30 "Diverging oscillating forecast for small imaginary parts" [formal; supporting; support: formal_proof E3; verification agent_checked; verifiability open]
  When $\alpha_0 \ge 0$ and $0 < \alpha_1 < 1 - \alpha_0$, the [[D1:CES]] point forecast is a diverging harmonic oscillation.
  in plain words: For a non-negative real part and small positive imaginary parts of the complex smoothing parameter, below one minus the real part, the CES forecast oscillates with growing swings.
  corrected from the published paper (correction; Section 3.3, the list of forecasting trajectories): printed When $0 < \alpha_1 < 1 - \alpha_0$ the diverging harmonic trajectory is produced.
C43 "Conditional variance of CES computed as for additive ETS" [formal; secondary; support: cited B1, at p. 96, own earlier work; assumes C60, C62, C63; uses C16; verifiability open]
  The conditional variance of [[D1:CES]] $h$ steps ahead, given the level and the [[D3:nonlinear trend component]], can be calculated from its state-space form in the same way as for pure additive ETS models.
  in plain words: The variance of a CES forecast several steps ahead, given the current states, is calculated from its state-space form as for pure additive exponential smoothing models.
== 4. The underlying ARMA(2,2) model
C5 "CES has an underlying ARMA(2,2) model with tied coefficients" [formal; principal; support: formal_proof E5; assumes C61; uses C16; verification agent_checked; verifiability open]
  [[D1:CES]] has an underlying ARMA(2,2) model whose coefficients $$\begin{aligned} \phi_1 &= 2 - \alpha_0 \\ \phi_2 &= \alpha_0 + \alpha_1 - 2 \\ \theta_1 &= 2 - 2\alpha_0 + \alpha_1 \\ \theta_2 &= 3\alpha_0 + \alpha_1 - 2 - \alpha_0^2 - \alpha_1^2 \end{aligned}$$ are functions of the same two parameters $\alpha_0$ and $\alpha_1$, so that the autoregressive and moving-average coefficients are tied together; $\theta_2$ is nonlinear in them.
  in plain words: CES can be written as an ARMA(2,2) model whose autoregressive and moving-average coefficients are all functions of the same two parameters, so they are tied together; one of the moving-average coefficients is nonlinear in them.
  extends B26: Exponential smoothing methods have equivalent ARIMA models: simple exponential smoothing corresponds to ARIMA(0,1,1).
C6 "The ARMA(2,2) of CES has a parameter space unlike the general ARMA(2,2)" [formal; principal; support: formal_proof E5; uses C5; verification agent_checked; verifiability open]
  The parameter space of the ARMA(2,2) model underlying [[D1:CES]] differs from that of the general ARMA(2,2) model.
  in plain words: The coefficient values the ARMA(2,2) model underlying CES can take are not the same as those of a general ARMA(2,2) model.
  extends B26: Exponential smoothing methods have equivalent ARIMA models: simple exponential smoothing corresponds to ARIMA(0,1,1).
  qualifies B27: The coefficients of a general ARMA(2,2) model are free parameters, restricted only by the stationarity and invertibility conditions.
C7 "The ARMA(2,2) of CES is not restricted to stationary models" [formal; principal; support: formal_proof E5; uses C5; verification agent_checked; verifiability open]
  The parameter space of the ARMA(2,2) model underlying [[D1:CES]] is not restricted to stationary models.
  in plain words: The ARMA(2,2) model underlying CES can take coefficient values that make it non-stationary, not only stationary ones.
  qualifies B27: The coefficients of a general ARMA(2,2) model are free parameters, restricted only by the stationarity and invertibility conditions.
== 5. Connection with simple exponential smoothing
C44 "At an imaginary part of one, CES is simple exponential smoothing" [formal; secondary; support: formal_proof E3; uses C20; verification agent_checked; verifiability open]
  When $\alpha_1 = 1$, [[D1:CES]] is simple exponential smoothing with smoothing parameter $\alpha_0 - 1$; when $\alpha_1$ is close to 1, it behaves like it over short horizons only.
  in plain words: When the imaginary part of the complex smoothing parameter is one, CES is simple exponential smoothing with smoothing parameter equal to the real part minus one; close to one, it behaves like it over short horizons only.
  corrected from the published paper (correction; Section 3.4.2): printed When $\alpha_1$ is close to 1 the influence of $\hat{e}_t$ on $\hat{y}_t$ becomes minimal and the second smoothing parameter $\alpha_0$ behaves similarly to the smoothing parameter in SES.
C45 "Real part near one for a stationary series" [formal; secondary; support: formal_proof E3; uses C44; verification agent_checked; verifiability open]
  When $\alpha_1$ is close to 1, the real part $\alpha_0$ of the [[D2:complex smoothing parameter]] of [[D1:CES]] is close to 1 for a stationary series, for which the smoothing parameter of simple exponential smoothing would be close to 0.
  in plain words: With the imaginary part near one, a stationary series, for which simple exponential smoothing would use a smoothing parameter near zero, gives CES a real part near one.
C46 "Real part near two for a random walk" [formal; secondary; support: formal_proof E3; uses C44; verification agent_checked; verifiability open]
  When $\alpha_1$ is close to 1, the real part $\alpha_0$ of the [[D2:complex smoothing parameter]] of [[D1:CES]] is close to 2 for a random walk.
  in plain words: With the imaginary part near one, a random walk gives CES a real part of its complex smoothing parameter near two.
== 6. Seasonal CES and model selection
C8 "Seasonal CES uses lagged states instead of seasonal indices" [methodological; principal; support: argument W1; limitations L2; warrant W1; verifiability open]
  [[D4:seasonal CES]] models seasonality with lag-$m$ level and nonlinear trend states instead of seasonal indices.
  in plain words: Seasonal CES represents seasonality by a second level and nonlinear trend taken one seasonal period back, rather than by a set of seasonal indices as conventional exponential smoothing does.
  scope: data_type: seasonal univariate time series | conditions: additive error; seasonal period m
  extends B1, own earlier work: Conventional seasonal exponential smoothing models represent seasonality with a set of seasonal components.
  extends B27, at p. 300: Seasonal ARIMA models describe seasonality through relations between observations a seasonal period apart.
C9 "Seasonal CES needs no renormalising of seasonal indices" [methodological; principal; support: argument W1; uses C8; warrant W1; verifiability open]
  [[D4:seasonal CES]] has no seasonal indices that need renormalising.
  in plain words: Because seasonal CES has no seasonal indices, it needs no step to renormalise them, which conventional seasonal exponential smoothing needs.
  scope: data_type: seasonal univariate time series | conditions: additive error; seasonal period m
  addresses B1, own earlier work: The seasonal components of conventional exponential smoothing models need to be renormalised.
C47 "Seasonal CES has an underlying SARIMA model" [formal; secondary; support: formal_proof E6; uses C8; verification agent_checked; verifiability open]
  [[D4:seasonal CES]] has an underlying SARIMA(2,0,2m+2)(2,0,0)$_m$ model.
  in plain words: Seasonal CES can be written as a seasonal ARIMA model with two autoregressive terms, $2m+2$ moving-average terms and two seasonal autoregressive terms, $m$ being the seasonal period.
C48 "Seasonal CES can be stationary or non-stationary" [formal; secondary; support: argument W4; uses C47; limitations L2; warrant W4; verification agent_checked; verifiability open]
  [[D4:seasonal CES]] is a stationary or a non-stationary model depending on the values of its complex smoothing parameters.
  in plain words: Like non-seasonal CES, seasonal CES is stationary or non-stationary depending on the values of its complex smoothing parameters.
C31 "Seasonal CES can produce nonlinearly changing seasonal amplitude" [methodological; supporting; support: argument W3; uses C25; warrant W3; verifiability open]
  [[D4:seasonal CES]] can produce seasonality whose amplitude changes nonlinearly.
  in plain words: Seasonal CES can produce seasonal patterns whose size grows or shrinks in a nonlinear way.
  scope: data_type: seasonal univariate time series | conditions: seasonal CES
C49 "Seasonal CES produces all trajectories of non-seasonal CES" [methodological; secondary; support: argument W3; uses C25; warrant W3; verifiability open]
  [[D4:seasonal CES]] can produce all the trajectories of non-seasonal [[D1:CES]].
  in plain words: Every forecast shape that non-seasonal CES can produce can also be produced by seasonal CES.
  scope: data_type: seasonal univariate time series | conditions: seasonal CES
C50 "Seasonal CES can model seasonal shapes with an unchanging level" [methodological; secondary; support: argument W3; uses C25; warrant W3; verifiability open]
  [[D4:seasonal CES]] can model multiplicative or other shapes of seasonality when the level of the series does not change.
  in plain words: Seasonal CES can model multiplicative or other shapes of seasonality even when the level of the series does not change.
  scope: data_type: seasonal univariate time series with a constant level | conditions: seasonal CES
C42 "AIC of CES from a Gaussian likelihood" [methodological; secondary; support: cited B1, at p. 68, own earlier work; assumes C60, C64; verifiability open]
  The [[D39:Akaike information criterion]] of seasonal and non-seasonal [[D1:CES]] can be computed from a Gaussian likelihood of the same form as that of additive exponential smoothing models.
  in plain words: The Akaike information criterion of seasonal and non-seasonal CES can be computed from a normal likelihood of the same form as for additive exponential smoothing models.
  scope: data_type: univariate time series | conditions: additive Gaussian errors
C51 "Non-seasonal CES has five estimated parameters" [methodological; secondary; support: argument W5; warrant W5; verifiability open]
  Non-seasonal [[D1:CES]] has 5 estimated parameters: two smoothing parameters, two initial states and the error variance.
  in plain words: Non-seasonal CES estimates five parameters: two smoothing parameters, two initial states and the error variance.
  scope: data_type: non-seasonal univariate time series | conditions: non-seasonal CES
C52 "Number of estimated parameters of seasonal CES" [methodological; secondary; support: argument W5; warrant W5; verifiability open]
  [[D4:seasonal CES]] has $4 + 2m + 2 + 1$ estimated parameters: four smoothing parameters, $2m$ initial lagged values, two initial non-seasonal states and the error variance.
  in plain words: Seasonal CES estimates four smoothing parameters, two initial values for each season of the period, two initial non-seasonal states and the error variance.
  scope: data_type: seasonal univariate time series | conditions: seasonal CES with seasonal period m
C10 "Model selection for CES is a choice between two models" [methodological; principal; support: argument W1; uses C8, C31, C13, C14, C15; limitations L2; warrant W1; verifiability open]
  Model selection for [[D1:CES]] reduces to choosing between the seasonal and the non-seasonal model.
  in plain words: Choosing a CES model reduces to choosing between its seasonal and non-seasonal versions, instead of among the many trend and seasonality forms of conventional exponential smoothing.
  scope: data_type: univariate time series, seasonal or non-seasonal | conditions: selection by an information criterion
  addresses B2: The trend alternatives of exponential smoothing in a state space framework make the selection of a model difficult.

WARRANTS
W1 [deductive; premises C8, C31, C13, C14, C15] The seasonal model takes the level and nonlinear trend states at lag m instead of carrying a set of seasonal components, so there are no seasonal indices to renormalise. Since non-seasonal CES captures both level and trend series without a choice of trend form, the only choice left is whether to include the lag-m states.
W2 [deductive; premises C20, C26, C27, C28] With the imaginary part of the smoothing parameter equal to one CES is the local level model ETS(A,N,N); with it above one the forecasts grow exponentially, as in multiplicative trend models; and the flexibility of its trajectories lets it follow trends that the level and trend classification of exponential smoothing captures only with separate models.
W3 [deductive; premises C25] Seasonal CES consists of two CES parts, one at lag 1 and one at lag m, and each part follows one of the CES forecast trajectories, so the seasonal part can grow, decline or oscillate in its own way. The non-seasonal level can become negative while the lagged level stays positive, and the two levels evolve independently, so the seasonal part can change its amplitude and shape without any change in the level of the series.
W4 [analogical; premises C47, C5] Non-seasonal CES has an underlying ARMA(2,2) model that is stationary or not depending on its complex smoothing parameter; by analogy, the authors state the same of the SARIMA model underlying seasonal CES. No stationarity condition is derived for seasonal CES.
W5 [deductive] The count follows from the model equations: each complex smoothing parameter has two real parts, each pair of level and nonlinear trend states needs initial values (m of each for the lag-m pair), and the error variance is estimated.
W6 [inductive] On the seasonal subset of the M1, M3 and tourism data, CES with its seasonal or non-seasonal form chosen by AICc has lower RMSSE and MASE than every seasonal ETS model, which the authors take to show that the criterion chooses the seasonal form when it is needed. This is an argument from the accuracy results only: no experiment compares the selection by information criteria with other ways of choosing between seasonal and non-seasonal CES.
W7 [inductive] CES has lower RMSSE than ETS, ARIMA and the Theta method on the M1, M3, tourism and M4 data and lowers the errors of the combination it is included in, which the authors take as evidence that it is robust, while acknowledging that it does not suit every forecasting scenario. Robustness itself is not defined or tested: no experiment examines how the accuracy of CES varies across conditions or departures from its assumptions.

LIMITATIONS
L1 [model_specification; affects C3, C36, C37, C38, C39, C40; could_bias_magnitude; mitigation none] CES produces very large errors on a few series: on M1 series 47 it fails to identify the trend and its MSIS is 523.034 against 25.590 for ETS(M,N,N), which inflates its mean MSIS, and on the non-seasonal subset it fails on two series.
L2 [model_specification; affects C8, C10, C47; reduces_precision; mitigation none] The parameter restrictions under which seasonal CES is stable are not derived.
L3 [sample_size; affects C3, C4, C11, C12; reduces_transferability; mitigation none] The effect of the length of a time series on the accuracy of CES is not analysed.
L4 [model_specification; affects C16; reduces_transferability; mitigation none] CES is formulated only with an additive error term; a version with a multiplicative error term is not developed.
L5 [sampling; affects C4, C12; could_bias_magnitude; mitigation none] The 10 M4 series on which ETS broke down were excluded from the M4 comparison, so the series were selected on the failure of one of the compared methods.

OPEN QUESTIONS
O1 [from C11] Whether CES improves the SCUM combination because it captures long-term relations better than ETS, ARIMA and the Theta method, and nonlinear trends without separating level and trend, is not established.
