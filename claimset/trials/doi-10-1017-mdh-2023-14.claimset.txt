DEFINITIONS
D1 [theory] segmented sleep thesis: Ekirch's thesis that people in pre-industrial western Europe routinely slept in two segments, a 'first' and a 'second' sleep separated by up to an hour or more of wakefulness, and that consolidated sleep is a modern artefact of artificial light. [source B1]
D2 [setting] early modern England: England in the sixteenth to eighteenth centuries, from which much of the thesis's textual evidence comes.
D3 [source] Ekirch's textual sources: The printed and manuscript passages quoted by Ekirch as references to first and second sleep: Bentley (1582), Taillepied, Erasmus (1535), Bayly, Davenant, Baldwin, Fidge, Gombauld, Shirley, Amyraut and others. [members D9, D10, D11] [source B1]
D4 [data] Wehr's photoperiod experiment: A study of 16 participants (15 men, one woman) aged 20 to 36, one week at 16 h light and 8 h dark then four weeks at 10 h light and 14 h dark, confined to a dark room in the dark period. [source B2]
D5 [method] EEBO full-text search: Full-text searches of Early English Books Online for 'first sleep' and 'second sleep' in English printed texts of 1475-1700, each hit read in context. [source B3]
D6 [method] close reading in context: Re-reading each quoted passage with its surrounding text, genre and circumstances of writing (illness, watch duty, prayer collections, figures of speech).
D7 [concept] first sleep: The early modern phrase whose meaning is at issue: a separate first segment of sleep, or the first, deepest or earliest part of a night's sleep, or an allocated watch.
D9 [source] Bentley (1582): A prayer book of 1582 quoted by Ekirch, with a prayer for waking at midnight.
D10 [source] Erasmus (1535): A letter of Erasmus of 1535 quoted by Ekirch.
D11 [source] Amyraut: A passage by Amyraut quoted by Ekirch, in which a sleeper wakes from a dream.
D8 [concept] unusual or unwanted waking: Waking at night described as troublesome, caused by illness, fear, duty or circumstance, rather than as a routine interval.

CLAIMS
C1 [interpretive; principal; support: empirical_qual E1; limitations L1; warrant W1]
  Most passages of [[D3:Ekirch's textual sources]] describe [[D8:unusual or unwanted waking]] rather than a routine interval between two sleeps in [[D2:early modern England]].
  disputes_evidence B1: Ekirch reads these passages as evidence that segmented sleep was routine.
C2 [interpretive; principal; support: empirical_qual E2; limitations L1, L2; warrant W2]
  In [[D2:early modern England]] the phrase [[D7:first sleep]] carried several meanings, including the first or deepest part of a continuous night's sleep and an allocated watch, so its occurrence does not show segmented sleep.
C3 [interpretive; principal; support: argument W3]
  [[D4:Wehr's photoperiod experiment]] does not support the [[D1:segmented sleep thesis]] for [[D2:early modern England]], because its participants lay in darkness without the candle and firelight of early modern evenings and its bimodal pattern was not quantified.

WARRANTS
W1 [interpretive] A passage shows routine segmentation only if it presents night waking as ordinary; passages written in illness, about watches, in emergency prayers or as figures of speech present it as exceptional.
W2 [interpretive] 'First' commonly meant initial or earliest; used of sleep it can name the first, deepest part of one sleep, as in Caryl, as readily as a separate segment, and only 5 of 15 'second sleep' hits name both.
W3 [analogical] An experiment informs a historical claim only as far as its conditions resemble the historical ones; 14 hours confined in darkness do not resemble evenings lit by candle and fire.

LIMITATIONS
L1 [researcher_position; affects C1, C2; reduces_transferability; mitigation none] The author states that any alternative model of early modern sleep is by nature limited and uncertain, and does not deny that segmented sleep existed.
L2 [sampling; affects C2; reduces_transferability; mitigation none] Hits for 'first sleep' were not counted, and EEBO covers printed English texts only, not manuscripts or speech.
